# Evidence: how to weigh what you find

The method follows the genealogical proof standard in plain terms: search broadly, cite
fully, analyse each source, resolve conflicts, and state the conclusion in writing.

## Label every source three ways

**Source type**
- `original`: the first record of the event, made at or near the time (a civil register entry).
- `derivative`: a copy, transcription, index, abstract or translation of an original.
- `authored`: someone's own account or compilation (a family history, an online tree).

**Information type** (per fact, not per source)
- `primary`: given by someone with first-hand knowledge, near the time (the father reporting a birth).
- `secondary`: given by someone without first-hand knowledge, or long after (a death record's
  birth date and parents' names).
- `undetermined`: you cannot tell who the informant was.

**Evidence type** (how the fact bears on the question)
- `direct`: states the answer outright.
- `indirect`: the answer follows only if you combine it with other facts.
- `negative`: the absence of expected evidence, after a search broad enough to expect it.

A single source can hold primary and secondary information. A death record is primary for the
death and often secondary for the birth.

## Confidence levels

| Level | Minimum |
|---|---|
| `confirmed` | One original record that states the fact directly with primary information, or two independent sources that agree, with no unresolved conflict |
| `probable` | A reasoned argument from indirect evidence; every other explanation tested and found weaker |
| `possible` | Fits the evidence; at least one other explanation is still open |
| `unverified` | A claim found only in authored or undetermined material |

Rules that stop most mistakes:
- A claim never rises above its weakest necessary link.
- Independence means different informants and different record creators. An index and the
  record it indexes are one source.
- Do not raise confidence because several trees agree.
- Lower the confidence when a conflict is found, and say so.

## Identity tests

Two records are the same person only if you can say why. Test each, and write the result
as `pass`, `fail` or `not testable`:

1. Name, allowing for spelling, prefixes and patronymics.
2. Age or birth date within the tolerance of the sources.
3. Place, including moves that make sense.
4. Spouse, children, parents.
5. Occupation and social setting.
6. Witnesses, godparents, neighbours.
7. Timeline: nothing happens before birth or after death.

If a test fails, the match is rejected unless an error in the record explains it and you say so.

## Plausibility limits (warnings, not laws)

- Mother at a child's birth: under 15 or over 50 is a warning.
- Father at a child's birth: under 15 or over 70 is a warning.
- A child born after the mother's death, or more than about nine months after the father's, is an error.
- Marriage under 15 is a warning.
- Age at death over 105 is a warning.
- Two events for one person on the same day in two far-apart places is a warning.

## Conflicts

When sources disagree, record every version in `conflicts`, with:
- what each source says,
- the source and information type,
- which version you adopt and the argument for it, or "unresolved".

Prefer: original over derivative; primary over secondary information; a record made near
the event over a later one; an independent source over one that may copy another.
Never delete the losing version.

## Negative evidence

A negative finding counts only if it comes with: the register or index searched, the
period covered, the name variants tried, and the reason you expected a hit. "No baptism
found in the church books of X for 1840 to 1850 under Jans, Jansen, Janssen" is useful.
"Nothing online" is not.

## Citation format

`[Creator or archive], "[Record title or type]", [place, date], [access path], accessed [date].`

birth record of Anna Voorbeeld, 1883, https://example.org/record/123, accessed 2026-11-03.
